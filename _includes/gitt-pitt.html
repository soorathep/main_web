Both techniques step the cell a little and wait, right across the state of charge, and return the chemical (apparent) diffusion coefficient of the working ion, the equilibrium (OCV) curve, and the resistance vs SOC. The absolute D is only trustworthy for comparing samples measured the same way — see the caveats.

GITT — galvanostatic (current pulse → rest)

Run: constant-current pulses (~C/20–C/10, 5–30 min) each followed by a rest to equilibrium (1–10 h, until dV/dt is flat). Keep current small & pulse short so E is linear in √t. Do charge and discharge.

Read four voltages per step: ΔEs = equilibrium change (E4−E1); ΔEτ = transient change during the pulse, IR removed (E3−E2).

D = (4 / π·τ) · ( m_B · V_M / (M_B · S) )² · ( ΔE_s / ΔE_τ )²      [ valid for  τ ≪ L²/D ]

τ pulse time · m_B, M_B mass & molar mass of active material · V_M molar volume · S electrode–electrolyte area. Also: OCV = rested voltage; overpotential η = Emeas − Eeq; internal resistance R = η / I.

PITT — potentiostatic (small voltage steps → current decay)

Run: a staircase of small steps (ΔE ≪ 25 mV, typically 5–20 mV); hold each until the current decays to a cut-off, then step again. Integrate current per step → dQ/dE and OCV.

Long-time diffusion limit: fit the linear tail of ln|I| versus time after faster modes have decayed, before background current or noise dominates. If the slope is s:

One-sided planar film (thickness L; blocking back): s = −π²D/(4L²) → D = −4L²s/π²
Homogeneous sphere (radius R; fixed surface):       s = −π²D/R²     → D = −R²s/π²

Both limits assume a small step, single-phase diffusion with nearly constant D, a fixed surface concentration after the step, and negligible charge-transfer and ohmic limitations. At short times, a diffusion-controlled current may follow |I| ∝ t^(−1/2) (Cottrell); obtaining D from that slope also requires a geometry-appropriate relation.

The transient shape or a dip in apparent D can suggest a change worth investigating, but neither identifies a phase transition without independent evidence.

gitt_pitt.py: The current script uses only the one-sided planar-film prefactor for PITT. Do not use a particle radius as its input length. Its synthetic demo has a prescribed dip in D.

Worked example (GITT)

Electrode: mB = 1.5 mg, MB = 96 g mol⁻¹, density 4.8 g cm⁻³ → VM = 20 cm³ mol⁻¹, area S = 1.13 cm² (12 mm disk), pulse τ = 600 s. One step gives ΔEs = 8 mV and ΔEτ = 40 mV.

m_B·V_M / (M_B·S) = (1.5e-3 · 20)/(96 · 1.13) = 2.77e-4 cm

D = (4 / π·600) · (2.77e-4)² · (8/40)²  ≈  6.5 × 10⁻¹²  cm² s⁻¹

Do this for every step to get D vs SOC. The script gitt_pitt.py (in the downloads) does it automatically from a data file — its demo produced the figure below.

Report & caveats
  • Report step size, duration, rest/cut-off criterion, temperature, direction, and how you got S or L (geometric vs BET vs particle size).
  • Show a representative single step (E–√t for GITT, ln|I|–t for PITT) and plot D vs SOC for charge and discharge — never one headline number.
  • The absolute D can be wrong by orders of magnitude (unknown true area / length); it is a relative measure. Call it apparent / chemical D.
  • The simple single-phase formulas are not valid across two-phase or conversion intervals. Check the fit window and geometry before comparing GITT and PITT.
